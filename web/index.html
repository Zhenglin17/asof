The timing rule: does it beat holding the same stocks?

Frozen on 2026-10-29 after at least 18 exploratory versions on the seven biggest tech stocks: buy when the weekly and daily B-Xtrender histograms turn up, the daily one at or above zero, and the weekly THT trend is green; sell on its exit signals. Each list is set against the same stocks held throughout and re-weighted on the days the rule trades, so the only difference is when the rule is in or out. Equal weights, 5 bps a trade, 2020-02-03 to 2025-06-30; shown is A-spread-equal, the variant with the highest return on each list (not the highest Sharpe: on the top 20 that is A-cash-equal, 0.83 against 0.79); none of the eight beats holding on the top 20 or without the seven.

What I would test next: fast falls against slow bears

A hunch, not a pattern yet. In 2022, the only slow bear in the sample and labelled after the fact, the timing rule fell less than holding on every list; in the fast falls of 2020, 2024-08 and 2025 it fell about as much or more on the top 20, and more on the seven in 2020 and 2025. A rule that sells and buys on confirmation would do this in a V-shaped fall (not yet checked trade by trade). The catch: on the day of a sharp fall nobody knows whether it is a V or the start of a slow bear, and price bars alone may not tell.

  • Data to try. The VIX term structure: in a shock, expected volatility over the next month jumps above that over three months (market quotes, not revised figures; whether it also inverted in early 2022 is unchecked). Macro data as first published (ALFRED), since revised figures leak the future. Dated news and central-bank statements, read by the same cited-evidence layer.
  • The limit. Six SPY falls of 8% or more since 2018 (2018-02, 10%, is the sixth), four inside the rule's window. A rule learned from them cannot be tested on them: it has to be argued in advance, checked on single-stock falls, which are far more numerous, and then on falls still to come.
  • Not built yet. Recorded as the next hypothesis, one attempt when it is run.

How it avoids seeing the future

A backtest that peeks is worse than none. Each way of peeking below has a mechanism in the code and a test that fails if it comes back.

A trap caught on the way

The SEC publishes its fund ticker table as of today, without dates, and tickers are reused. Today FB is a ProShares fund; until 2022 it was Facebook. Classed by today's table, Facebook was an ETF from 2020-01 to 2022-06, so no stock scan ever saw it and the top-20 list had a hole at number 5. The fix: a ticker counts as a fund only when the listing has no company id (CIK) or the fund's own; an audit now lists fund-class listings of companies that file 10-Ks.

Market cap = SEC share count public by that night × that night's close. Visa and Berkshire Hathaway report no all-class share count and are left out, so the 19th and 20th names here would rank lower in a full list.
